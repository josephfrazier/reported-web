Semi-Automatic Mode

Upload a folder of photos — or just a pile of loose files — covering many violations at once. The app runs ALPR and reverse-geocoding over the whole batch, groups the photos into violations, and starts walking you through them one at a time as soon as the first settles — form pre-filled, ready to submit, while the rest of the photos are still being read.

Grouping algorithm

A new pure module, src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.js, so the whole algorithm is unit-testable without React. In goes one record per processed photo; out comes one record per violation. 

in:  [{ file, name, createDateMs, latitude, longitude,
        plateResults, uploadWidth, uploadHeight }]
out: [{ photos[], plate, licenseState, latitude, longitude, createDateMs }]

The four passes

  1. Sort photos by capture time, so everything downstream can assume chronological order. Missing timestamps fall back to file last-modified and sort last.
  2. Cluster by time. Union two photos when Δt ≤ 60 s. Place plays no part; see Why distance went. Connected components become candidate violations.
  3. Vote the plate, then split on it. Fold OCR variants together, count how many photos contain each plate, and take the winner. Photos containing it become a violation; the remainder is recursed on. This is what stops two cars photographed back-to-back from merging into one report.
  4. Adopt orphans. Photos with no plate read at all join whichever sub-cluster is nearest in capture time, so a blurry shot still rides along with its violation. Finally, sort the violations earliest-first.

Why distance went

Distance was the second half of the rule until it was measured against the reports themselves. Two numbers came out of that, and neither supports it.

Of 385 gaps between two photos of one violation, 328 were at identical coordinates. A phone reuses one location fix for shots taken seconds apart. Most violations therefore carry a single coordinate, and distance has nothing to compare.

The rest cannot be told from GPS error. The photos are taken from a bike, so long distances inside one violation are real: 713 m over 180 s is 14 km/h, and 1,130 m over 96 s is 42 km/h on an e-bike. But the same population holds 4,739 m in 2 s and 1,798 m in 2 s, which no bike covers. A threshold low enough to separate two violations splits the real ones. One high enough to keep them together separates nothing.

So the plate does that job instead. It has to: the pairs a threshold cannot divide are the ordinary case, not the awkward one.

The 60 s window comes from the same measurement. Across 399 gaps inside one violation, 5 s split 33 of them and 60 s splits 2. The largest real gap was 180 s.

Which photo gives the place

A report needs one time and one place, and they come from opposite ends of the group.

The time is the earliest photo's, because that is the moment the report describes.

The place is the last photo's that carries a fix. A camera woken up for the shot can still be reporting the fix from wherever it was last used, so the first reading of a group is the one most likely to be stale, and the last is the one taken once the GPS had caught up with the car. The time cannot follow the same way: a report is of when the violation happened, not of when its last photo was taken.

Both rules hold for any set of photos rather than for a whole violation only, so neither lives in the grouping. The place is latestLocatedPhoto and the time is earliestTakenPhoto, one small module each. The grouping asks them once, to record what a violation is, and the form asks the same two whenever the photos a report holds change — so a violation means the same thing in the queue as it does in the form.

Choosing the plate

Each tie-break is applied only if the one before it is still tied:

  1. Most photos
  2. Most central
  3. ^T\d{6}C$
  4. Highest score
  5. The group the earliest photo voted for
  6. Alphabetically first, so the same batch always groups the same way

Most central means the smallest mean normalised distance between the plate's box centre and the frame centre, across the photos it appears in. Boxes arrive in the uploaded image's pixel space, and alpr.js already attaches uploadWidth and uploadHeight to the response — normalising against those lets a 4096-wide and a 2048-wide photo be compared fairly. The medallion pattern survives as an extra tie-break, preserving the intent of issue #584.

Why variants must be folded

The checked-in ALPR snapshot reads one medallion four different ways in a single image. Counting exact strings, that is four plates with one photo each — so a two-photo violation would look like two violations sharing no plate, and the vote that picks the plate would have nothing to pick.

t696817c t6968i7c t696b17c t696bi7c

Plate Recognizer hands back its own ranked alternatives in candidates, so folding is a union-find over plate strings whose edges come from candidate-set intersection — no invented edit distance. Note only the clean read matches ^T\d{6}C$, which is exactly why that pattern cannot be the primary signal.

Worked example

Six photos from one walk down a block. Photos 1–3 are the same medallion read three ways; 4–5 are a second car; 6 is a shot where ALPR failed.

# EXIF time Δt From prev. Plate read Violation
108:14:02—— t696817c V1
208:14:064 s3 m t6968i7c V1
308:14:115 s2 m t696817c V1
408:14:2918 s144 m lda8765 V2
508:14:334 s2 m lda8765 V2
608:16:40127 s154 m none V3

Photo 6 → V3 breaks on time: 127 s is past the window, and a photo with no plate at all is flagged rather than discarded. Photos 3 → 4 do not break — 18 s is inside the window — so both cars sit in one cluster, and the plate vote is what divides them. That is the ordinary case rather than the awkward one: two cars at one kerb are 0 m and a few seconds apart, and nothing but the plate can tell them apart. Within V1 the three readings fold into one plate with three photos — the majority it wins is t696817c, the clean read and the only one matching the medallion pattern.

Order of operations matters

Clustering runs before the plate vote, and the plate vote runs before the split. The plate cannot merge two clusters that time kept apart — it only ever divides. That keeps the rule one-directional and avoids the obvious circularity of using a plate both to define a group and to be chosen from it.

When a violation is final

The queue fills in while the batch is still being read, which needs a rule for when a violation has stopped changing. It is the linking window itself rather than a photo count: since linkPhotos requires two photos to be within 60 s of each other, once the frontier — the latest photo read so far — has moved more than that past a violation's end, nothing still to come can reach back and join it. isViolationSettled is that test, exported and unit-tested on its own because it is the whole trigger.

Two things follow from it. The batch's last violation can never settle on its own — settling needs a later photo to pass it, and there is none — so a final pass publishes whatever is left. And nothing needs freezing explicitly: a published violation settled before it was published, so nothing read since can have changed it, and its photos are left out of later grouping passes. That is what holds the queue's order, and the index it is pointing at, still — the violation being worked on cannot shift underneath the form.

Dates first, then plates

The batch reads each photo twice. The first pass takes the date and the place out of the file's own metadata: local work, no requests, so it is quick. The second reads plates, and it runs in the order the first pass established.

That order cannot come from the files' modification times. They stand in for capture time only where the two agree, and on the batch that prompted this they did not: two photos of one car were read either side of a photo from a different violation, so the car became two reports. Sorting the batch by the date each photo actually carries is what makes the boundary in the section above exact rather than probable — every photo not yet read sits at or after it.

Caching

The assumption that ALPR results are already cached is half right, and the half that is wrong is the half that matters.

ResultTodayNeeded
ALPR attachmentPlateCache, a WeakMap keyed on the File object Nothing new. The queue holds the same File objects, so hits are automatic
Reverse geocode A memo keyed on the coordinates (geosearchAddressCache.js), which setCoords reads before asking Nothing new — the batch geocodes each violation through the same memo
Server side Nothing. Both endpoints call through unconditionally Nothing — but see the note on content hashes

Because the ALPR cache keys on object identity rather than content, it only helps while the same File objects are alive. A batch queue holds them for its whole run, which is why no new ALPR caching is required — but a page reload loses everything, matching the decision above.

Geocode once per violation, not per photo

An address is a function of coordinates alone. A batch taken at one curb used to cost one identical lookup per photo, and the memo already brings that down to one; grouping first means the form only ever asks for the violation it is showing, and never for the ones it has not reached.

A ready-made key, if results ever need to persist

Every upload already gets a SHA-256 of its bytes (attachmentId), and identical uploads share a temp file. Nothing consults that hash for results today — the bytes go to Plate Recognizer again even when the hash matches. If reload-survival is ever revisited, that hash is the key to build on.

Module & state

New file

src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.js plus a colocated src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.test.js, following the repo's habit of extracting logic into small pure modules with direct tests.

Two helpers live alongside it:

  • normalizedCenterDistance(box, uploadWidth, uploadHeight) — how far a plate sits from the frame centre
  • isViolationSettled({ violation, frontierTimeMs }) — whether a violation has stopped changing, which is what publishing waits for

Capture order, and the two rules that read it, live in src/latestLocatedPhoto.js: effectiveTimeMs, compareByCaptureTime and hasCoordinates, and the place a set of photos gives. The time is src/earliestTakenPhoto.js. The grouping imports them and so does the form, so both sort and read a set of photos the same way.

Do not reuse handleAttachmentData

It re-runs extraction over the entire attachment array on every add. Pointed at a batch, that re-walks every photo each time a violation is loaded. The batch needs its own one-shot pass.

Two existing functions to get right

  • fetchPlateResults — the batch must use this, because it returns the full response including every results[] entry and is the function the WeakMap already wraps.
  • extractPlate — deliberately not used by the batch. It collapses each photo to a single plate and prefers the medallion pattern, which discards exactly the multi-plate data the grouping needs. The single-violation flow keeps its current behaviour.

Submitting a violation

getPerSubmissionState() already returns precisely the per-submission fields — plate, licenseState, latitude, longitude, coordsAreInNyc, formatted_address, CreateDate, typeofcomplaint, reportDescription, can_be_shared_publicly — and is already wired into the submit payload. Loading a violation sets them through the existing setLicensePlate, setCreateDate and setCoords.

Worth knowing: the name reads backwards. This repo's bundled object.omit (v3) treats its predicate as a keep filter, so despite saying omit the helper returns only those keys.

typeofcomplaint is never auto-filled from a photo — it stays manual, as does reportDescription.

Interface

Ways in

Four inputs feed the same batch, so photos can arrive however they happen to be sitting on disk — in a directory of their own, loose in a camera roll, dragged straight from a file manager, or copied to the clipboard:

  • Add a folder — <input type="file" multiple webkitdirectory>
  • Add photos — a plain <input type="file" multiple>, with no parent directory required
  • Drag and drop — the page is already wrapped in a Dropzone, so dropped files can feed the batch too
  • Paste — an image copied to the clipboard joins the batch like any other file

The three picks read event.target.files directly rather than going through the existing FileReaderInput. That component is used with as="buffer", which reads every selected file's contents into memory; across a whole folder that is a great deal of buffering before ALPR has even started. (For the record it would accept webkitdirectory — it spreads unknown props onto the real input — it is the buffering that rules it out.)

Picks accumulate, so a folder and then a few stragglers make up one batch. Selection order does not matter: grouping sorts by EXIF time, never by the order files arrived in.

What the toggle changes

With the mode on, every one of these routes feeds the batch, a paste included. The single-violation flow is what the toggle-off state gives you. Because a folder pick, a loose multi-select, a drop and a paste all take the same path, there is never a question of which flow a given pick lands in.

During the batch

An inline <progress> bar on the form, counting photos read. No modal: the batch is long-running and the user should be able to see the page while it works.

The bar names the two passes over the files themselves — dates and places, then plates. Grouping and geocoding happen per violation as it is published, so those are not stages of their own; the queue filling in underneath is the progress for them.

The queue

Violations appear as they settle rather than when the batch ends (see When a violation is final), so reporting can start while the rest of the photos are still being read. Each row shows its time, plate, photo count and flags, and clicking a row's own text opens that violation; Delete drops a row, and a row's checkbox ticks it for a merge. There is no button to walk the queue in order, because Submit already advances to the next violation and any row is one click away.

Tick two or more rows and Merge appears on the queue's heading. It makes one report of them, which the grouping cannot do for itself: it splits on time, and the photo that establishes where a car is can sit minutes before the closeups that read its plate, so the wide shot becomes a violation of its own. Only the user knows the two are one report, and merging takes their word rather than re-running the clusterer, which would split them apart again. The merged report keeps the plate of the group with the most photos, and takes its time and place from all of the photos it now holds.

The first violation loads itself, so the form is ready to submit without a click. Loading one puts its photos in the form as thumbnails with the usual plate overlays, and fills the fields from what the batch already extracted; Submit then advances automatically. Loading the violation already loaded is a no-op rather than a reload, because loading resets the attachment selection to the group's default and would discard a swap made with the picker. Only the photos that went into the report leave the batch when it is submitted: a group's leftovers appear at once as a row of their own, so a photo left out with the X is not lost.

Groups over three photos

The picker shows every photo in the group with checkboxes, the first three pictures and the first three videos selected. It must enforce 3 images and 3 videos, because that is the server's real limit — createSubmission.js takes images.slice(0, 3) and videos.slice(0, 3) and drops the rest without an error. Today the only hint is the button label, and the dropzone enforces nothing at all.

A report takes its time and place from the photos it is holding, so unticking one moves both onto the photos that are left. The X on a thumbnail does the same, which matters because a group of three or fewer has no picker at all. Either is left alone once the user has set it by hand — a time typed into the field, or a place the pin was dragged to.

Tests

The algorithm gets direct unit tests; the flow is exercised through the existing Home harness.

  • groupAttachmentsByViolation.test.js — variant folding, plate-driven splitting, the 60-second window, and each fallback (no GPS, no time, no plate).
  • Home.test.js — driving the rendered batch UI to assert that violations are found, that the first loads itself as it settles, that loading one fills the fields from cache, that no second /platerecognizer call fires, and that Submit advances.
  • Publishing — isViolationSettled is unit-tested at both edges of the window, and it is worth watching what each publish pass left in state, since a pass that ran while the progress bar was still up is the whole feature.

The best fixture is already checked in. The ALPR snapshot holds exactly the variant case that would break a naive exact-match vote, so a test built on it fails loudly if folding regresses.

Running them

Use yarn test:no-flaky for the suite CI runs. It leaves out only alpr.test.js and getVehicleType.test.js, so geoclient.test.js and srlookup.test.js still call live services and need a network. Run those deliberately, and always through yarn test, never npx jest — the -r dotenv/config in the yarn script is the only thing loading credentials, so under npx they fail misleadingly.

Risks

  • Rate limits and quota Two different limits, pulling in opposite directions. POST /platerecognizer carries no server-side rate limit, so a batch of N photos is N ALPR calls fired in parallel, and the concurrency cap is politeness rather than enforcement. Plate Recognizer rate limits back, and a refused read is retried up to four times with a doubling wait (plateReadRetry.js), so a batch that trips the limit spends several calls per photo rather than one. Check the account's own quota before running a large batch. /api/uploadAttachment is the opposite — capped at a quarter of a gigabyte of unsubmitted attachments per IP, which a submission releases as it consumes them (attachmentBudget.js). A client that uploaded every file it picked would exceed that at once, and every upload past it would be refused quietly, leaving the submit to send those files itself. That fallback is correct, but it means the pre-upload optimisation stops helping exactly when a batch is large enough to need it — so the queue uploads a report's photos when it loads that report, not the folder it was picked from.
  • Silent 3 + 3 truncation Extras past three images and three videos vanish with no error. The picker has to enforce this or photos disappear between review and submission.
  • Violations outside NYC coordsAreInNyc gates the submit button. Those groups should be flagged in the queue rather than discovered at Submit.
  • Repeated duplicate warnings Reports from one batch resemble each other, so the existing duplicate-submission warning will likely fire over and over as the queue is walked.
  • Videos in scope Supported today (frame-0 grab for ALPR) and assumed included. Say so if they should be excluded from the batch.

Implementation

Three commits, in this order — the algorithm is pure and independently reviewable before any UI touches it.

  1. The module. groupAttachmentsByViolation.js and its tests. Pure, no React, no UI.
  2. The geocode memo. A "lat,long" → address cache, with its own test, so repeated lookups stop re-hitting NYC Planning Labs.
  3. The wiring. Batch state, the three photo inputs, the progressive publishing pass, the queue, load-next, the picker and auto-advance in Home.js, plus the Home.test.js coverage.
