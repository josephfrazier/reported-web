Grouping algorithm

A new pure module, src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.js, so the whole algorithm is unit-testable without React. In goes one record per processed photo; out comes one record per violation. 

in:  [{ file, name, createDateMs, latitude, longitude,
        plateResults, uploadWidth, uploadHeight }]
out: [{ photos[], plate, licenseState, latitude, longitude, createDateMs }]

The five passes

  1. Sort photos by capture time, so the queue walks the batch in the order it was shot. Missing timestamps fall back to file last-modified and sort last.
  2. Fold the plate strings. Plate Recognizer ranks its own alternatives in candidates, so the four ways it reads one medallion are one plate. Folding is a union-find over strings, with an edge for every pair a single result offers.
  3. Give each photo its own plate. A photo can read several plates — a closeup of one car with another parked behind it. Its own plate is the result the read named: a medallion result if the read offered one, and the first result otherwise. That is the rule the form applies to the same read, so the queue row and the field agree. The folded group of that result is the photo's plate group.
  4. Merge photos that name the same plate. Every photo starts as a violation of its own. A photo joins the row of every other photo whose folded plate group it names, unless it reads another plate that a different photo also reads. That photo is evidence for two cars, and giving it to one row would take it from the other, so it stays a row of its own.
  5. Split on the report's cap. A report carries three pictures and three videos at most, so a violation over either count becomes consecutive groups, in capture order, that fit. Every photo of a violation reads the plate it reports, so each part keeps that plate. A row the user merged by hand is not split. Finally, sort the violations earliest-first.

A photo ALPR read nothing in is a violation of its own, flagged for manual entry. The user can merge it with its car by hand, or a second-chance read can merge it later.

Why time and place went

Capture time and place were the first grouping signals. The reports themselves show that neither can tell two cars apart.

The ordinary case is two cars at one kerb: 0 m and a few seconds apart. Of 385 gaps between two photos of one violation, 328 were at identical coordinates, because a phone reuses one location fix for shots taken seconds apart. The rest cannot be told from GPS error. The photos are taken from a bike, so long distances inside one violation are real: 713 m over 180 s is 14 km/h, and 1,130 m over 96 s is 42 km/h on an e-bike. But the same population holds 4,739 m in 2 s and 1,798 m in 2 s, which no bike covers. A threshold low enough to separate two violations splits the real ones. One high enough to keep them together separates nothing.

Time fails the same way. Across 399 gaps inside one violation, a 5 s window split 33 and a 60 s window split 2. The largest real gap inside one violation was 180 s. A window short enough to keep two cars apart splits the photos of one car; a window long enough to keep one car together pulls two cars in. The plate is the only signal that names the car, so it is the only signal that groups.

Which photo gives the place

A report needs one time and one place, and they come from opposite ends of the group.

The time is the earliest photo's, because that is the moment the report describes.

The place is the last photo's that carries a fix. A camera woken up for the shot can still be reporting the fix from wherever it was last used, so the first reading of a group is the one most likely to be stale, and the last is the one taken once the GPS had caught up with the car. The time cannot follow the same way: a report is of when the violation happened, not of when its last photo was taken.

Both rules hold for any set of photos rather than for a whole violation only, so neither lives in the grouping. The place is latestLocatedPhoto and the time is earliestTakenPhoto, one small module each. The grouping asks them once, to record what a violation is, and the form asks the same two whenever the photos a report holds change — so a violation means the same thing in the queue as it does in the form.

Choosing the plate

The tie-breaks below choose the spelling a row prints inside its plate group, and which group wins when more than one is offered. Each is applied only if the one before it is still tied:

  1. Most photos
  2. Most central
  3. ^T\d{6}C$
  4. Highest score
  5. The group the earliest photo voted for
  6. Alphabetically first, so the same batch always groups the same way

The photo's own group is not chosen by this list: the read's result order decides it, with a medallion result first. The list chooses the string within that group. The spelling read by more photos comes first, then the spelling whose boxes are most central, then the clean medallion read, then the highest score.

Most central means the smallest mean normalised distance between the plate's box centre and the frame centre, across the photos it appears in. Boxes arrive in the uploaded image's pixel space, and alpr.js already attaches uploadWidth and uploadHeight to the response — normalising against those lets a 4096-wide and a 2048-wide photo be compared fairly. The medallion pattern survives as an extra tie-break, preserving the intent of issue #584.

Why variants must be folded

The checked-in ALPR snapshot reads one medallion four different ways in a single image. Counting exact strings, that is four plates with one photo each — so two photos of the car would never name the same plate, and each would stay a row of its own.

t696817c t6968i7c t696b17c t696bi7c

Plate Recognizer hands back its own ranked alternatives in candidates, so folding is a union-find over plate strings whose edges come from one result's own candidate set — no invented edit distance. Note only the clean read matches ^T\d{6}C$, which is exactly why that pattern cannot be the primary signal.

Worked example

Six photos from one walk down a block. Photos 1–3 are the same medallion read three ways; 4–5 are a second car; 6 is a shot where ALPR failed. Photo 1 also catches the second car, which is why its row stands alone.

Photo 1 reads the medallion, the lda8765 car and a third car. Its own plate is the medallion — the read's medallion result — but the lda8765 read is shared with photos 4–5, so the frame is evidence for two cars and stays a row of its own. Photos 2–3 name the same folded medallion group, and the spelling their row prints is t696817c, the clean read and the only one matching the medallion pattern. Photos 4–5 name the second car. Photo 6 reads nothing, so it is a row of its own with no plate, flagged for manual entry.

One photo starts one row

Photo 1's frame reads three cars, but a photo is one violation until a plate joins it to another. When two of its plates have closeups of their own, as here, the frame belongs to neither row: it is evidence for both, so it stays a row of its own. The user can merge it into either car's report by hand.

One shared plate per photo

A photo merges only when its own plate — the read's first result, or its medallion result — is the one plate it shares with another photo. A second shared plate means the photo shows two cars, and giving it to one row would take it from the other. That guard is what keeps a wide shot from chaining two cars into one report. The plate both defines the row and labels it, and the guard is what makes that safe.

When the rows appear, and what changes them

The dates and places pass is local and it covers the whole batch, so once it is done every photo can be a row: one photo is one violation until a plate says otherwise. The queue is published there and then — a second or so after the folder is picked, whatever the batch size and whatever the rate limit is doing. The plate reads start as soon as the rows are out; the address lookups wait for the first read to be on the wire, so the free lookups follow the scarce plate calls.

The plate reads merge the rows in behind it. Each read replaces its photo's record and the whole batch regroups: photos whose folded plates agree become one row, and a photo that turns out to read two shared plates leaves the row it was in. That is the same grouping applied as soon as it can be, so a car's photos join up as its readings arrive.

The form waits a little longer than the queue. The first row is loaded once its own photos have been read; a row that a later read merges into it is loaded again with the merged row's photos, so the report is not left short of its own car's evidence. A row that divides under the form is loaded again onto the row holding the photos it had attached.

What this replaced was a rule for when a violation had stopped changing — the frontier and isViolationSettled, published one violation at a time only once nothing still to come could reach back and join it. Every row now starts as one photo, so the queue can appear at once and settle as the reads merge it.

Dates first, then plates

The batch reads each photo twice. The first pass takes the date and the place out of the file's own metadata: local work, no requests, so it is quick. The second reads plates, and it runs in the order the first pass established.

That order cannot come from the files' modification times. They stand in for capture time only where the two agree, and on the batch that prompted this they did not. Sorting by the date each photo actually carries gives the queue its order and gives each report its time. It also lets the rows be published before any plate is read: every photo is a row at that point, and the date is what orders it.

Caching

ALPR results are cached twice: a WeakMap in the page, and a disk cache on the server. Geosearch answers have two layers as well: the page's coordinate memo, and a disk cache on the server. Each layer answers a repeated request without another call.

ResultTodayNeeded
ALPR attachmentPlateCache, a WeakMap keyed on the File object Nothing new. The queue holds the same File objects, so hits are automatic
Reverse geocode A memo keyed on the coordinates (geosearchAddressCache.js), which setCoords reads before asking. A fallback is shown but not kept Nothing new — the batch geocodes each violation through the same memo, and does not keep a fallback either
ALPR, server side alprCache.js, keyed by the SHA-256 of the uploaded bytes; on by default, and off when ALPR_CACHE_DIR is set to nothing Nothing new
Geosearch, server side geosearchCache.js, keyed by the coordinates and the selected address, normalized; on by default, and off when GEOSEARCH_CACHE_DIR is set to nothing Nothing new

The page's cache keys on object identity rather than content, so it only helps while the same File objects are alive. A batch queue holds them for its whole run. A page reload loses the queue, but the server's disk cache still answers the same bytes without another call while it is enabled.

Geocode once per violation, not per photo

An address is a function of coordinates alone. A batch taken at one curb used to cost one identical lookup per photo, and the memo brings that down to one. The lookups start once the first plate read is on the wire, so no free geosearch request runs in front of a plate read — the reads own the scarce quota and supply the grouping. A row that opens before its address is in asks for the same place itself, and that answer fills the memo for the rows after it. The memo and the batch's in-flight set keep its own repeated places from being fetched twice; the form's debounced lookup is separate, so a row opened before the batch's lookup answers can ask for the same place once more. A reload clears the page's memo, and the server's disk cache answers a place it has seen before without calling geosearch. An answer that fell back to the top result because the address directory was down is shown but not kept, in the page's memo or on disk. geocodePlaces warms the memo for every distinct place while the plate reads run.

The hash is the key

Every upload gets a SHA-256 of its bytes (attachmentId), and identical uploads share a temp file. The ALPR disk cache uses the same trick: alprCacheKey hashes the bytes the request carries, so a repeated photo is answered without calling Plate Recognizer. The cache is on by default outside tests; ALPR_CACHE_DIR moves it, and an empty value turns it off.

Module & state

New file

src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.js plus a colocated src/groupAttachmentsByViolation.test.js, following the repo's habit of extracting logic into small pure modules with direct tests.

It exports the grouping itself and four helpers:

  • normalizedCenterDistance(box, uploadWidth, uploadHeight) — how far a plate sits from the frame centre
  • plateForPhotos(photos, { foldFrom }) — the plate a set of photos reports; the queue row and the form both call it
  • plateCropFor({ photos, plate }) — the photo, box and frame size the queue draws the plate crop from
  • mergeViolations(violations) — the hand merge the queue's Merge button performs

Capture order, and the two rules that read it, live in src/latestLocatedPhoto.js: effectiveTimeMs, compareByCaptureTime and hasCoordinates, and the place a set of photos gives. The time is src/earliestTakenPhoto.js. The grouping imports them and so does the form, so both sort and read a set of photos the same way.

Grouping telemetry

The batch records what the user corrects: a hand merge, a deleted row, a photo split into a new violation, a photo moved to another row, a discarded queue, a duplicated row, and a sampled batch-done event. Each event carries the raw photo records and the rows before and after.

src/batchGroupingEvents.js is the seam. The browser entry registers a Sentry sender, and Home reaches it through captureBatchGroupingEvent. The module imports no Sentry code, so the server bundle can import it too; with no sender — the server, tests, or a build without a DSN — the calls do nothing. The sender attaches the payload as batch.json, because the event's own field limits would trim it.

Submitting a violation

getPerSubmissionState() already returns the per-submission fields — email, FirstName, LastName, Phone, plate, plateProvenance, licenseState, latitude, longitude, coordsAreInNyc, formatted_address, CreateDate, typeofcomplaint, reportDescription, can_be_shared_publicly, withhold_contact_info_from_nypd, receive_nypd_sr_updates_by_email, testify — and is already wired into the submit payload. Loading a violation sets them through the existing setLicensePlate, setCreateDate and setCoords.

Worth knowing while reading the file: this repo's bundled object.omit (v3) treats its predicate as a keep filter, so despite saying omit the helper returns only those keys. getPerSubmissionState spells its own filter out rather than relying on that; the rest of the file still uses omit where the name fits.

typeofcomplaint is never auto-filled from a photo — it stays manual, as does reportDescription.

Interface

During the batch

An inline <progress> bar above the queue, counting photos read. No modal: the batch is long-running and the user should be able to see the page while it works.

The bar names the two passes over the files themselves — dates and places, then plates. Grouping happens with the first of them, and the queue appearing is that progress. The plate reads start as soon as the rows are published; the batch's places are geocoded behind them, so a row can open before its address is in. The bar's second pass is the plate labels filling in and the rows merging.

The queue

The rows appear as soon as the batch's dates and places are in — before a single plate is read (see When the rows appear) — so reporting can start while the rest of the photos are still being read. Each row shows its time, its plate or whether it is still being read, its photo count and flags, and clicking a row's own text opens that violation; Delete drops a row, and a row's checkbox ticks it for a merge. The plate a row names is the plate the form opens with, and the overlay selected in the photo is that same plate. There is no button to walk the queue in order, because Submit already advances to the next violation and any row is one click away.

Deleting a row takes its photos out of the batch with it, except a photo another row still holds: a copied row shares its photo with its source, so deleting one leaves the photo for the other. The deleted row itself is remembered, so a later regrouping cannot make it again.

The Merge button sits on the queue's heading whenever two or more rows are shown, and is enabled once two of them are ticked. It makes one report of them, which the grouping cannot do for itself: it merges only what the plates agree on, and a far shot taken to establish where a car is can read no plate, while a read can fail. One car can therefore stand as two rows. Only the user knows the two are one report, and merging takes their word rather than re-running the grouping, which has no signal that says so. The merged report keeps the plate of the row with the most photos among those that read a plate, and takes its time and place from all of the photos it now holds.

Opening a row with nothing ticked ticks that row, so putting it together with the row beside it is one tick rather than two. While fewer than two rows are ticked, the tick follows the row the form shows: opening another row moves it. Once two or more are ticked — a merge the user is building — opening another row leaves the selection as it is.

A row's identity — what a tick names, and what the list's own keys are made of — is its time, its plate and the photo it opens with. A batch shot at one kerb gives several rows the same second, so the time alone does not name one; the first photo and the plate do. A copy agrees with its source on all three, so it carries a copy key as well, and the key is what names it.

A photo dragged to the New violation target normally leaves its old row and becomes a violation of its own. A row of one photo has no other photo to be named by, so the target copies the row instead and leaves the source as it was: the same photo then backs two reports. The copy is marked as the user's own, so a later read leaves it whole, and both rows show the shares a photo badge.

The first violation loads itself, so the form is ready to submit without a click. Loading one puts its photos in the form as thumbnails with the usual plate overlays, and fills the fields from what the batch already extracted; Submit then advances automatically. Loading the violation already loaded is a no-op rather than a reload, because loading resets the attachment selection to the group's default and would discard a swap made with the picker. Only the photos that went into the report leave the batch when it is submitted: a group's leftovers appear at once as a row of their own, so a photo left out of the picker is not lost. The X on a thumbnail is the other action: it deletes the photo from the batch.

Groups over three photos

The grouping splits a violation that is over the report's cap before it reaches the queue (see The five passes), so an ordinary row already fits. A violation is one photo unless its plate joins it to others, so only a car with more than three pictures or three videos reaches the cap; its photos then become consecutive reports that fit, all under the same plate.

Every loaded row's photos carry a checkbox, whatever the count, so a photo can be left out of the report without leaving the batch; the first three pictures and the first three videos are ticked. A row the user merged by hand can hold more: a fourth picture or video can be ticked so that its thumbnail can be seen, and the warning says to uncheck one to swap it in. Submit stays off until the report is back within 3 images and 3 videos, which is the server's real limit — createSubmission.js takes images.slice(0, 3) and videos.slice(0, 3) and drops the rest without an error, so the report cannot be filed over the cap.

A report takes its time and place from the photos it is holding, so unticking one moves both onto the photos that are left. The X on a thumbnail deletes the photo from the batch instead, and moves them the same way. Either is left alone once the user has set it by hand — a time typed into the field, or a place the pin was dragged to.
